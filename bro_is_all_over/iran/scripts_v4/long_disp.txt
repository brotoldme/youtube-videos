Popular accounts say that twenty-four hundred years ago, people in the Iranian desert were making ice.
In summer. With no electricity. No machines.
Some of those ice houses are still standing. The oldest ones left are younger, but the idea is ancient.
And that is only one reason to visit Iran. I'll give you seven. One is a desert whose ground satellites rank the hottest on Earth. And the secret behind that ancient ice is still hidden underground.
This is Iran. Close to ninety million people. The capital is Tehran.
Reason one: Isfahan. Its great square, Naqsh-e Jahan, means pattern of the world.
It is five hundred sixty meters long, with two mosques, a palace, and the entrance to a bazaar around it. It was built between fifteen ninety-eight and sixteen twenty-nine.
Reason two: Persepolis. Darius the Great began this ceremonial capital around five eighteen B C.
Alexander the Great captured it in three thirty B C, but its stairways and carved stone reliefs still stand. And in the southwest, a ziggurat from about twelve fifty B C is among the best preserved in the world.
Reason three: Nowruz, the Persian New Year. It means new day, and it is more than three thousand years old.
It starts on the first day of spring and lasts thirteen days. Before it, families clean the whole house, and many people jump over small fires.
Reason four: the food. Start with tahdig, the crispy golden rice from the bottom of the pot. The word means exactly that.
Add saffron. Iran grows close to ninety percent of the world's supply.
And for dessert, faloodeh, in Shiraz: frozen noodles, rose water, and lime. That is where the ice went.
Reason five: Yazd. The whole old city is built of mud brick, and people still live in it. UNESCO listed it in twenty seventeen.
Look up, and you will see tall towers that catch the breeze and pull cool air down into the house. The tallest, at Dowlat Abad Garden, is almost thirty-four meters high.
But Yazd gets almost no rain. So where does its water come from?
Reason six: the qanat. It is a tunnel that starts at the foot of a mountain, where water hides in the soil.
It slopes just a little, and gravity does the rest. The water travels kilometers, with no pump, and no sun to dry it up.
The Gonabad qanat has four hundred twenty-seven shafts and over thirty-three kilometers of tunnel. It was dug more than twenty-five hundred years ago. And it still delivers water.
That is the secret. The same water filled ice pools, cooled houses, and fed gardens. UNESCO lists eleven of these qanats.
Reason seven: extremes. Mount Damavand, a volcano higher than fifty-six hundred meters, is the tallest volcano in Asia. And it is capped with snow.
Then the Lut Desert, whose name means bare, empty land. In two thousand five, a NASA satellite measured one hundred fifty-nine degrees Fahrenheit on its ground. A later NASA study of eighteen years of data ranked it the hottest surface on Earth, tied with a desert in Mexico.
Canada currently advises avoiding all travel to Iran. When that changes, this is where to start.
