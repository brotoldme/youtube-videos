Twenty-four hundred years ago, people in the Iranian desert were making ice.
In summer. With no electricity. No machines.
They served frozen desserts, in a place where July heat tops forty degrees.
And some of those buildings are still standing.
Today, Iran is short of water. And the answer may be sitting under its own desert.
I'll show you three inventions. One makes ice. One cools a house. And one drinks from a mountain. The third is the secret behind the other two.
This is Iran. Close to ninety million people. The capital is Tehran.
Invention one: the yakhchal. In Persian, it means ice pit.
It is a giant dome of mud brick, with a deep cellar underneath.
Beside it stands a tall shade wall. Shallow pools sit in that shade.
On freezing winter nights, the clear desert sky pulls the heat out of the water. By morning, it is ice.
Workers cut the ice, packed it in straw, and stored it in the cellar. A chimney at the top let warm air escape, so the cellar stayed cold all summer.
Popular accounts date this idea to about four hundred B.C. The oldest buildings still standing are much younger. But the idea is ancient.
That ice went into faloodeh, a frozen dessert of thin noodles, rose water, and lime. People in Shiraz still eat it today.
But ice was a luxury. Everyone else just had to survive the heat.
Invention two: the badgir, a wind catcher.
Look at the rooftops of Yazd, and you will see tall towers. About seven hundred of them.
Each tower catches wind high above the city, and sends cooler air down into the house. The hot air leaves through slots. The house breathes.
The tallest one, at Dowlat Abad Garden, is almost thirty-four meters high.
Yazd is now a UNESCO World Heritage city. But Yazd gets almost no rain. So where did its water come from?
Invention three: the qanat.
A qanat is a tunnel. It starts underground, at the foot of a mountain, where water hides in the soil.
The tunnel slopes just a little, and gravity does the rest. The water travels kilometers, with no pump, and no sun to evaporate it.
The Gonabad qanat has four hundred twenty-seven shafts, over thirty-three kilometers of tunnel. It was dug more than twenty-five hundred years ago. And it still delivers water.
That water filled the ice pools. It cooled the houses. It fed the gardens. One invention powered the other two.
So what went wrong? In recent decades, Iran built about six hundred dams, and drilled deep wells.
Qanats were left behind. About thirty-three thousand still work, down from fifty thousand. Hydrologists say about half of the systems have run dry.
And the dams? In November twenty twenty-five, the five reservoirs feeding Tehran averaged around ten percent full. The president has warned that the capital may have to move.
The ancient system kept desert cities alive for twenty-five centuries. Whether the modern one can do the same is still an open question.
