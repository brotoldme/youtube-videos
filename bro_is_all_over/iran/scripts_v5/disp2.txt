Twenty-five hundred years ago, one empire ruled almost half the people on Earth. By Guinness's count, no empire has ever ruled a bigger share.
Its ceremonial capital stood in Iran. And you can still climb its stairs.
Those stairs are carved with delegations from twenty-three nations, all bringing gifts to the king.
That is reason number one to visit Iran. There are eight. A great square. A New Year over three thousand years old. And one place so hot, a satellite measured one hundred fifty-nine degrees Fahrenheit on its ground.
This is Iran. Close to ninety million people live here. The capital is Tehran. And more than twenty-five of its places are on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
Reason one: Persepolis. Darius the Great began it around five eighteen B C. Alexander the Great captured it in three thirty B C, but its stairways and carved reliefs still stand.
Drive south and you reach Chogha Zanbil. It is a stepped temple tower, called a ziggurat, built around twelve fifty B C. That is older than Persepolis by about seven hundred years.
Reason two: Isfahan. Its great square, Naqsh-e Jahan, means pattern of the world. It is five hundred sixty meters long, with two mosques and a palace around it.
It was built between fifteen ninety-eight and sixteen twenty-nine. Today, it is where locals sit on the grass and have a picnic in the evening.
Reason three: Shiraz. Walk into the Nasir al-Mulk Mosque just after sunrise. Light pours through the stained glass, and colored patches slide across the carpets. Locals call it the Pink Mosque, for its tiles.
Nearby, the Persian Garden is a UNESCO listing of nine gardens in nine provinces. Its idea is simple: a shaded paradise, with running water, in the middle of a dry land.
Reason four: Nowruz, the Persian New Year. It means new day, and it is more than three thousand years old. It starts with the first day of spring and lasts thirteen days.
Families clean the whole house and set a table with seven items starting with the letter S. Many people jump over small fires. On day thirteen, the whole country picnics outside.
Reason five: the food. Start with tahdig, the crispy golden rice from the bottom of the pot. The word means exactly that.
Add saffron. Iran grows around ninety percent of the world's supply. And for dessert, faloodeh, in Shiraz: frozen noodles, rose water, and lime.
Reason six: extremes. Mount Damavand, a volcano higher than fifty-six hundred meters, is the tallest volcano in Asia. And it is capped with snow.
Then the Lut Desert. Its name means bare, empty land. In two thousand five, a NASA satellite measured one hundred fifty-nine degrees Fahrenheit on its ground. That is the ground, not the air.
A later NASA study of eighteen years of data ranked it the hottest surface on Earth, tied with a desert in Mexico.
Reason seven: Yazd. The whole old city is built of mud brick, and people still live in it. UNESCO listed it in twenty seventeen.
Look up, and you will see tall towers that catch the breeze and pull cool air into the house. The tallest, at Dowlat Abad Garden, is almost thirty-four meters high.
But Yazd gets almost no rain. So where does its water come from?
From a qanat. It is a gently sloping tunnel that carries mountain water for kilometers, with no pump.
The Gonabad qanat has four hundred twenty-seven shafts and over thirty-three kilometers of tunnel. It was dug more than twenty-five hundred years ago, and it still delivers water. UNESCO lists eleven of them.
Reason eight: the people. Say salaam. It means hello, and it comes from the word for health. Thank you is mamnoon. Goodbye is khodahafez, may God protect you.
Dress the way locals do. Women wear a headscarf and loose clothing. Men skip the shorts. The rules apply to tourists too.
