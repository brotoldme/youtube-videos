For centuries, the world called this country Persia.
Then, in nineteen thirty-five, its king asked everyone to use a new name: Iran.
He picked one date for the change. March twenty-first. The first day of spring.
I will tell you why at the end. First, let me show you a country you will want to visit.
This is Iran. About ninety-two million people live here. The capital is Tehran.
The language is Persian, and you say hello with one word: salaam. Dozens of other languages are spoken too.
Prices are spoken in tomans, but the real currency is the rial. One toman equals ten rials.
Now, four facts that will surprise you. Fact one. Nearly ninety percent of the world's saffron comes from Iran.
Fact two. In winter, you can ride a gondola from Tehran up to a ski resort, more than thirty-seven hundred meters high.
Fact three. Not far away, Mount Damavand is the highest volcano in Asia.
Fact four. In the Lut Desert, satellites have measured sand at over seventy degrees Celsius. That is one of the hottest places ever recorded on land.
And yet, people here made ice in the desert, over two thousand years ago. They built giant clay cones called yakhchals, and kept ice frozen all summer.
Now, where would you go? Start in Isfahan. An old Persian saying calls it half the world.
Its great square, Naqsh-e Jahan, is more than five hundred meters long, and it is one of the largest squares on Earth.
Next, go south, to Shiraz. In the Nasir al-Mulk Mosque, called the Pink Mosque, come early in the morning. The sun shines through stained glass and paints colors on the carpet.
A short drive away stands Persepolis, founded by Darius the Great in five eighteen B C. By Guinness's count, around four eighty B C this empire ruled forty-four percent of all people on Earth.
Then Yazd, a city of mud brick in the desert. Tall towers catch the breeze and cool the houses.
Now, let us eat. The national dish is chelow kabab: grilled meat, with rice.
But ask any Iranian about tahdig. The word means the bottom of the pot. It is the golden, crunchy crust of rice, and everybody wants a piece.
For dessert, try faloodeh, a frozen dessert of thin noodles. Yes, noodles. It was made with ice from those old ice houses.
Then there are the carpets. Persian weavers dye the wool with plants, like madder and indigo, even walnut skin and pomegranate skin. UNESCO listed Persian carpet weaving in twenty ten.
Poetry matters here too. In Shiraz, people visit the tomb of the poet Hafez. Many Iranians ask his book a question about the future. They open it at random, and the page gives the answer.
But the best time to visit is spring. Nowruz means new day. It begins at the exact moment of the spring equinox, and it is more than three thousand years old.
First, families clean the whole house. On the last Tuesday night of the year, people jump over small bonfires, to leave the old year's bad luck behind.
Then comes the table. The haft-seen: seven things whose names all start with the Persian letter S. Sprouts, an apple, garlic, vinegar, and sumac.
The new year lasts thirteen days. On the thirteenth, almost the whole country goes outside for a picnic.
Now you can see why the king chose March twenty-first. Nowruz, the first day of spring, is Iran's oldest holiday.
And the name Iran was not new. Iranians had used it for centuries, while the world said Persia. In nineteen thirty-five, the king simply asked the world to use it too.
Did the world obey? Not entirely. In nineteen fifty-nine, his son said both names were fine. We still say Persian carpets, and the Persian language.
