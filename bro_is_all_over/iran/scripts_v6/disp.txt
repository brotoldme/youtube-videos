For centuries, the world called this country Persia.
Then, in nineteen thirty-five, its king asked foreign governments to use a different name: Iran.
He picked one date for the change. March twenty-first. The first day of spring.
Why that day? To find out, you need three things: an empire, a poem, and a table set with seven things.
This is Iran. Nearly ninety million people live here. The capital is Tehran.
First, the empire. Around five fifty B C, a king named Cyrus rose in Pars, a region in the south of today's Iran.
The Greeks called that region Persis. The Romans turned it into Persia. And the name stuck, for the whole country.
By Guinness's count, around four eighty B C this empire ruled forty-four percent of all people on Earth. No empire has ruled a bigger share.
Its ceremonial capital, Persepolis, still stands near Shiraz. On its great staircase, twenty-three nations march up with gifts for the king.
But here is the catch. Persia came from the name of just one province. Inside the country, people used another word for the whole land.
That word is Iran. It is carved in stone on a cliff near Persepolis, in the third century A D. So the name is at least seventeen hundred years old.
So how did the name survive? Because the empire did not.
In six fifty-one, Arab armies ended the last Persian empire, and much of the old Persian culture was at risk of being lost.
Three centuries later, a poet named Ferdowsi started writing a book. It took him about thirty years.
The Shahnameh, the Book of Kings, has fifty thousand rhyming couplets, in Persian, from legend to the last empire. It is the longest epic poem written by a single author.
Scholars credit it with keeping the Persian language alive. And his tomb, in Tus, is still a place people visit.
So far: an empire, and a poem. The third thing is the one that explains the date.
Nowruz means new day. It begins at the exact moment of the spring equinox, around March twenty-first.
It is more than three thousand years old, and UNESCO listed it in two thousand nine.
First, families clean the whole house. On the last Tuesday night of the year, people jump over small bonfires, to leave the old year's bad luck behind.
Then comes the table. The haft-seen: seven things whose names all start with the Persian letter S. Usually sprouts, an apple, garlic, vinegar, and sumac.
Each one is a wish. Sprouts for rebirth. An apple for beauty. Garlic for health.
The new year lasts thirteen days. On the thirteenth, almost the whole country goes outside for a picnic.
Now you can see why the king chose March twenty-first. A name Iranians had used for seventeen centuries, asked of the world on their oldest holiday.
Did the world obey? Not entirely. In nineteen fifty-nine, his son said both names were fine. We still say Persian carpets, and the Persian language.
If you go, you can follow this whole story on the ground. Isfahan's great square, Naqsh-e Jahan, is a UNESCO site, built around sixteen hundred.
And it is a country of extremes. Mount Damavand is a snow-capped volcano about fifty-six hundred meters high.
In the Lut Desert, a NASA satellite once measured the ground at one hundred fifty-nine degrees Fahrenheit.
