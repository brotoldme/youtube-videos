For centuries, the world called this country Persia.
Then, in nineteen thirty-five, its king asked foreign governments to yooz a different name: Iran.
He picked one date for the change. March twenty-first. The first day of spring.
Why that day? To find out, you need three things: an empire, a poem, and a table set with seven things.
This is Iran. Nearly ninety million people liv here. The capital is Teh rahn.
First, the empire. Around five fifty B C, a king named Sigh russ rose in Pars, in the south.
The Greeks called that region Per sis. The Romans turned it into Persia, and the name stuck.
By Guinness's count, around four eighty B C this empire ruled forty-four percent of all people on Earth.
Its ceremonial capital, Per sep oh lis, still stands near Shee rahz. On its great staircase, twenty-three nations march up with gifts for the king.
But Persia was the name of just one province. Inside the country, people used another word for the whole land: Iran.
It is carved in stone on a cliff near Per sep oh lis, in the third century A D. So the name is at least seventeen hundred years old.
So how did the name survive? Because the empire did not.
In six fifty-one, Arab armies ended the last Persian empire, and much of the old culture was at risk of being lost.
Three centuries later, a poet named Fer dow see spent about thirty years on one book.
The Shah nah meh, the Book of Kings, has fifty thousand rhyming couplets. It is the longest epic poem written by a single author.
Scholars credit it with keeping the Persian language alive.
An empire, and a poem. The third thing explains the date. But first, two practical things, in case you ever go.
Money. Ten dollars is about twenty-seven million ree ahls, on the open market. The ree ahl is falling fast, so always check the rate.
For ten dollars, a metro ride costs about ten cents. A cheap lunch, around three fifty. A short ride in a ride-hailing app, one to four dollars.
A dinner for two in a mid-range restaurant? About seventeen dollars. So ten dollars covers most of a meal.
Now, the don't list.
Don't photograph government buildings, police, or military sites.
Don't count on cards. Foreign cards don't work in Iran, so bring cash.
Don't ignore the dress code. It applies to visitors, starting at the airport.
Don't carry alcohol or drugs. Both are illegal, even for tourists.
And don't take a driver's first offer of a free ride. That's tah rof. He expects you to insist on paying.
Now, the third thing. Noh rooz means new day. It begins at the exact moment of the spring equinox, around March twenty-first.
It is more than three thousand years old, and Yoo-nes-co listed it in two thousand nine.
First, families clean the whole house. On the last Tuesday night of the year, people jump over small bonfires, to leave the old year's bad luck behind.
Then comes the table. The haft seen: seven things whose names all start with the letter S. Sprouts, an apple, garlic, vinegar, and soo mak.
Each one is a wish. Sprouts for rebirth. An apple for beauty. Garlic for health.
The new year lasts thirteen days. On the thirteenth, almost the whole country goes outside for a picnic.
Now you can see why the king chose March twenty-first. A name Iranians had used for seventeen centuries, asked of the world on their oldest holiday.
Did the world obey? Not entirely. In nineteen fifty-nine, his son said both names were fine. We still say Persian carpets.
